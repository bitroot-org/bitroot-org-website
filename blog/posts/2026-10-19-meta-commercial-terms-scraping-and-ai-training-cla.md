**Meta's new commercial terms briefly let it use content from business websites to train its AI, and the status of that clause is now disputed.** [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/17/meta-removes-language-that-raised-scraping-and-ai-training-concerns/) that Meta has removed the provision, citing The Verge. Legal experts had already warned it could let Meta scrape any publisher with a business account. For a founder with a Meta ad account or business page, the lesson is larger than one clause: read the terms your platforms impose, because they can grant more than you expect.
 
## What the Clause Said
 
**The language covered any domain tied to a business account.** According to [Nieman Lab's reporting](https://www.niemanlab.org/2026/10/metas-new-commercial-terms-will-allow-the), the terms authorized Meta to use automated software to "crawl" and "collect" content, and to "use any content from such domains, web pages, or URLs to provide, develop, improve, research, train." The clause covered domains "associated with" a user's products or services.
 
**It was tied to a future date.** Nieman Lab reported the terms taking effect October 30. Business accounts could not opt out in advance, and the update told them to stop using Meta's products for commercial purposes if they disagreed.
 
**Experts read it broadly.** Cornell law professor James Grimmelmann said the terms let Meta train AI on any content from publishers' sites. Loevy & Loevy attorney Matt Topic said it was unclear whether a court would enforce it, but publishers should assume it would be. Benjamin Sobel of the University of Wisconsin compared the arrangement to "tying," because access to business products required accepting the scraping permission.
 
## What's Still Unclear
 
**Meta says the clause doesn't change its crawling.** A Meta spokesperson told TechCrunch the consolidated crawling terms "only applied to our ads and business products," and that the update "does not change how Meta uses web crawlers." Meta also pointed publishers to robots.txt as a control.
 
**Experts doubt robots.txt will settle it.** Grimmelmann and others said the new language could let Meta argue that its commercial terms override a robots.txt block. Meta declined to say whether the "test credentials" in the clause could bypass paywalls.
 
**The reporting conflicts on timing.** The Nieman Lab piece describes the clause as scheduled for October 30. TechCrunch, citing The Verge, says it has been removed. I couldn't open The Verge's report to confirm the removal, so check Meta's current terms directly before you act on either version.
 
## Reading the Terms You Accept
 
**Business terms can outlast your attention.** Most founders accept platform terms once, during signup, and never revisit them. A clause can change with a new effective date, and the notice may be routine.
 
**Look for four phrases.** Search any platform's terms for "crawl," "scrape," "train," and "any content." Those words signal permission to collect or reuse your material, often beyond what the product needs.
 
**Check what your tools can see.** Pixels, tag managers and business integrations can read your pages and forms. Know which vendors can access that data and whether their terms allow training.
 
## Judging a "Clarification"
 
**Compare the statement with the wording.** Meta's explanation says the crawling terms covered only ads and business products. The clause, as Nieman Lab quotes it, referred to any content from a business's domains. Those two readings aren't the same, and the gap is where your risk sits.
 
**Ask for the change in writing.** If a vendor says a term doesn't mean what it says, request that clarification in the contract itself, not just in a press statement.
 
## Scraping Law Context
 
**The leading ruling favors logged-off scraping.** In *Meta Platforms v. Bright Data*, Judge Edward Chen granted summary judgment to Bright Data on January 23, 2024. He wrote that "Bright Data did not 'use' Facebook and Instagram when it engaged in public logged-off scraping." The court did not address logged-in data or other websites, and I couldn't confirm the appeal status, so check before relying on it. ([Freeman Mathis & Gary summary](https://www.fbm.com/raven-quesenberry/publications/major-decision-affects-law-of-scraping-and-online-data-collection-meta-platforms-v-bright-data/))
 
**Terms and law are different tools.** Platform terms can bind your own use. The Bright Data ruling shows that a contract claim can fail for logged-off collection, but that doesn't make all scraping lawful.
 
## Vendor and Contract Checks
 
**Audit each vendor's content rights.** For every tool that touches your site, list what it can collect, whether it can train models on that data, and how to opt out.
 
**Write your own customer terms carefully.** If customers upload content, state what you may use, store, and train on, and keep the grant no broader than your product needs.
 
**Keep a review date.** Put a calendar reminder to review your top five platform terms every quarter, since the clauses that matter most can change with little notice.
 
## Recommendation Blocks
 
**Review terms closely if:**
- You run ads, a business page, or a pixel on a Meta property
- Your site holds content that publishers or competitors could reuse
**Negotiate or switch vendors if:**
- A tool's terms let it train on your customers' data
- The vendor can't say in writing what it may collect
**Focus on customer terms if:**
- Customers upload content into your product
- You plan to use customer data to train models
## A Decision Framework
 
**Step 1: Inventory platforms.** List every platform and vendor that accesses your site, data or customers.
 
**Step 2: Search the terms.** Check each for crawl, scrape, train and content-use language.
 
**Step 3: Record the status.** Note the effective date and whether any clause has been changed or removed.
 
**Step 4: Control access.** Use robots.txt where it helps, and limit tags and integrations to what you need.
 
**Step 5: Get it in writing.** Ask vendors to confirm training and crawling rights in their contracts.
 
## Bottom Line
 
**Meta's clause shows how platform terms can reach your content, even when the product itself seems harmless.** Meta says the change doesn't alter its crawling, and the clause's status is reported differently across outlets. Whatever Meta does next, the practical step is the same: read the terms your platforms impose, check what your vendors can collect, and keep those rights in writing.
 
This isn't legal advice. Talk to counsel before you change your customer terms or rely on a court ruling.
