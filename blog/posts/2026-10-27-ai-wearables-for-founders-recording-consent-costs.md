**AI wearables can capture every meeting, but recording a conversation can create a legal problem before it creates a useful note.** Devices from Amazon's Bee, Plaud and others record or summarize conversations, and their pricing mixes hardware with monthly fees. Before you wear one into a customer call, check the recording rules for every participant's location, the real annual cost, and whether the company behind the device will still support it. This post covers all three.
 
## What Is on the Market
 
**Bee's new models are the clearest current launches.** Amazon launched the Bee Frontier at $74.99 and the Bee Eclipse at $99.99 on October 15, 2026. [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/15/amazons-bee-launches-two-new-personal-ai-devices-starting-at-75/) that both show an indicator light while summarizing and offer optional Alexa connection.
 
**Plaud sells a note-taker with transcription.** Plaud's [product page](https://plaud.ai/intersection) lists the NotePin from $159 and the Note Pro from $189. The page also lists 300 free transcription minutes per month and 1,200 minutes on the Pro plan.
 
**Some popular products are no longer available to new buyers.** Meta acquired Limitless, the pendant formerly called Rewind, in December 2025. [TechCrunch reported](https://www.techcrunch.com/2025/12/05/meta-acquires-ai-device-startup-limitless/) that the company would stop selling its hardware, maintain support for existing customers for a year, and move subscribers to an unlimited plan. A late-October roundup still lists Limitless as a current product, so check before you buy.
 
## Consent Comes First
 
**Federal law is one-party, but states differ.** A [BuildBetter summary](https://blog.buildbetter.ai/ai-notetaker-lawsuits-2026-how-to-record-meetings-legally/) describes federal law as one-party consent, with California as an all-party state. It lists other all-party states, but its two lists disagree on some states, so confirm the current list with counsel. This is a vendor's blog, not legal advice.
 
**Assume the strictest rule applies.** For a call with participants in several states, the summary advises treating the strictest participating state's rule as governing the whole call. If anyone is in the EU or UK, data-protection law may also require a lawful basis before you process their voice.
 
**Give notice in more than one way.** The same summary recommends notice through a calendar line, a verbal statement and a chat message. It calls the calendar line weak on its own. If someone objects, don't record that call.
 
**Handle voiceprints separately.** Speaker identification creates biometric data, and the summary says verbal recording consent does not cover it. Illinois is named as a state where written notice and a release may be needed.
 
**Lawsuits show what can go wrong.** Otter.ai faces a consolidated privacy suit in California, where a court partly denied its motion to dismiss in August 2026. Fireflies faces Illinois biometric claims, and Granola faces a proposed class action over recording without disclosure. These are allegations, and the summary reports no liability findings.
 
## What the Devices Really Cost
 
**Bee has a free tier and a paid tier.** Bee's free tier gives summaries and 14 days of memory. [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/15/amazons-bee-launches-two-new-personal-ai-devices-starting-at-75/) that Bee Pro costs $11.99 per month and adds unlimited summaries, integrations and a personal chat assistant. A later roundup gave $19 per month, so confirm the current price on the product page.
 
**Plaud's monthly price depends on billing.** Plaud lists its Pro plan at $17.99 per month billed monthly, or $8.33 per month billed annually at $99.99 per year. A year of Plaud Pro therefore costs $99.99 on top of the $159 device, which is more than the device price.
 
**Run the annual number.** Add the hardware, the subscription for twelve months, and any accessories. Compare that total with the hours the tool saves, not the hardware price alone.
 
## Companion Devices and Brand Risk
 
**Companion wearables carry reputational risk.** Friend, a pendant that listens constantly and sends proactive messages, ran a New York subway campaign that founder Avi Schiffmann said cost more than $1 million. [Content Grip reports](https://www.contentgrip.com/friend-nyc-subway-campaign/) that commuters defaced the posters within days. The campaign ran in 2025, and the company's claim of about 400 units sold per week is the company's own figure.
 
**Treat "companion" claims as product claims.** A device that tracks social context or mood raises questions for the people around the wearer, not only for the buyer. If you deploy one in a team setting, decide who consents and who sees the output.
 
## Launch and Delivery Risk
 
**Check whether the product will ship.** Meta has introduced Muse Charm, a pocket assistant that has not shipped and has no confirmed price in the roundup I read. Nirva's shipping timeline is set for year-end.
 
**Limitless shows acquisition risk.** A pendant that stops selling after an acquisition leaves existing owners with support and data-export deadlines. Before you buy, check the company's status, its support commitment and its data export options.
 
**Verify launch claims.** The late-October roundup reports launch problems for the Rabbit R1 and gives a Sandbar ring date that doesn't match an October article. Confirm each product's status on the vendor's site.
 
## Recommendation Blocks
 
**Consider a wearable for meetings if:**
- Your meetings are internal, with participants in one state or consenting in advance
- You can keep a no-recording rule for customer calls with objections
**Consider a desktop or software recorder instead if:**
- Your team meets with customers in all-party states or the EU
- You need speaker identification, which raises biometric questions
**Wait before buying if:**
- The product's company has been acquired or stopped sales
- The device hasn't shipped and has no confirmed price
## A Decision Framework
 
**Step 1: Map your recording needs.** List which meetings you'd record and who attends from each location.
 
**Step 2: Set a consent rule.** Give notice in writing and verbally, honor objections, and turn off speaker identification where biometric consent is needed.
 
**Step 3: Price the year.** Add the hardware and twelve months of subscription, then compare that with the hours saved.
 
**Step 4: Check the company.** Confirm the product is still sold, supported and shipping.
 
**Step 5: Review with counsel.** Get written guidance for your states, countries and customer contracts before you record customer calls.
 
## Bottom Line
 
**AI wearables are useful for personal notes, but consent and cost decide whether they're worth using in business.** Check the recording law for every participant, price the year rather than the device, and confirm the product is still sold and supported. For customer calls, a written consent process matters more than the hardware.
 
This isn't legal advice. Talk to counsel about recording rules in your states, countries and contracts before you deploy any device.
