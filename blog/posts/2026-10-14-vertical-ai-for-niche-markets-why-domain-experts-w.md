**Most SaaS founders chase large markets. Niobia is building AI for a market its own co-founder calls "extremely niche."** The San Francisco startup places AI agents on battery and chip production lines to catch defects and help engineers find causes. Its founders are chemistry PhDs who previously worked at chipmakers and battery manufacturers. For bootstrapped founders, the lesson isn't about manufacturing. It's about how a narrow, expert-led market can work.
 
## What Niobia Actually Does
 
**Niobia puts AI agents on the production line itself.** According to [TechCrunch's coverage](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/13/niobia-takes-the-guesswork-out-of-some-of-techs-most-expensive-mistakes/), the agents run on self-contained GPUs mounted on manufacturing equipment, and they can spot pinhole-sized flaws such as scratches. Human engineers then investigate the likely causes, and the data is gathered in one place so they can query it.
 
**The models are open source, adapted to the domain.** Niobia runs open-source models that it trains further on battery and chip research data. This is the strategy that matters most for a small team: start from a capable open model and add the data only a domain specialist would know to collect.
 
**Niobia's current focus is narrow on purpose.** Its priorities are film coating, electroplating, and fluidized bed reaction, per the same article. A company that names three processes is choosing depth over breadth.
 
**What's not public matters too.** The coverage doesn't disclose funding, revenue, customer names, or pricing. Any founder evaluating this company should treat those as open questions.
 
## Angle 1: Domain Expertise as the Moat
 
**Both founders spent years inside the industry they now sell to.** Gaurav Jha and Harveen Kaur both hold chemistry PhDs and worked at chipmakers and battery manufacturers. They know what a yield problem costs, who signs the purchase order, and why engineers distrust generic tools.
 
**The data supports a correlation, not a guarantee.** Euclid Ventures reviewed [vertical software exits above $100M](https://insights.euclid.vc/p/how-founder-backgrounds-correlate) over five years. Teams with an industry insider among their co-founders accounted for 73% of exits by deal count and 84% of gross exit dollars. The author warns that this shows correlation, not cause, and that the data includes no baseline share of insiders among all founders.
 
**Another survey points the same direction.** An [Omers Ventures article](https://www.omersventures.com/news/vertical-saas-is-on-the-verge-of-a-massive-ai-transition-but-itll-be-guided) reports that 64% of early-stage vertical software startups were started by founders with direct experience in the sector. It argues that hands-on experience, networks, and intuition are hard for AI to replicate.
 
**For a SaaS founder, the practical test is simple.** If you cannot name three problems your target buyer has that a generalist would miss, you don't yet have the moat. Spend time inside the buyer's workflow before you spend money on features.
 
## Angle 3: Niche Economics
 
**A small market can still hold a large business.** Jha says the battery and chip market is "extremely niche; maybe people will never know about it." That's the same description many founders would use to dismiss an idea. The alternative reading: fewer buyers, higher stakes, and less competition for attention.
 
**The cost of failure sets the ceiling.** Niobia's coverage describes defects that can cost "millions in downtime and scrapped materials." For context, Siemens' [True Cost of Downtime 2024 report](https://assets.new.siemens.com/siemens/assets/api/uuid:1b43afb5-2d07-47f7-9eb7-893fe7d0bc59/TCOD-2024_original.pdf) puts unplanned downtime at about $2.3 million per hour in automotive, and as low as $36,000 per hour in FMCG. The report contains no semiconductor figure, so don't use it as a chip-industry number.
 
**Price against the failure, not the category.** When a product prevents an expensive problem, the buyer compares your price with that loss, not with other software budgets. That's a stronger position than competing on seats in a crowded category.
 
**The trade-off is a long sales cycle.** Niche buyers are few, and each relationship takes time. A bootstrapped founder can survive that only if the account value is high enough to fund the work.
 
## Angle 5: Open Models Lower the Build Cost
 
**Adapting an open model costs far less than training from scratch.** [Pricing data from February 2026](https://pricepertoken.com/fine-tuning) lists fine-tuning training costs of $0.48 per million tokens for Llama 3.1 8B on Together AI, and $2.90 for Llama 3.1 70B. By comparison, the same page lists $25 per million tokens for training GPT-4o, and $3 for GPT-4o-mini.
 
**Those are token prices, not project budgets.** They exclude data preparation, evaluation, deployment, and the engineering time required to keep a model accurate. Treat them as a floor for model cost, not the full bill.
 
**Open models also change the privacy conversation.** Jha says "none of our customers want their proprietary data to go to the cloud, even if we tell them it's safe." Running models on hardware the customer controls answers that objection directly, which is why Niobia lets customers hold their own encryption keys.
 
## Recommendation Blocks
 
**Build a niche vertical product if:**
- You have worked inside the buyer's industry and can name its specific failure costs
- Your buyers' problems are expensive enough that a few accounts can fund the business
- You can access domain data that a generalist competitor cannot
**Stay horizontal if:**
- You don't have a specific buyer in mind yet
- Your product's value depends on broad consumer or developer adoption
- You can't commit to a long sales cycle with few accounts
**Use open models if:**
- Your domain data is the differentiator, not the base model
- Your customers require data to stay on their own infrastructure
## Bottom Line
 
**Niobia's story is a manufacturing story, but the business pattern applies to any founder.** Domain expertise, a narrow buyer, and a costly failure can make a small market valuable. Open models make the build cheaper, and customer-controlled deployment can remove a major sales objection.
 
Be careful with the evidence. The exit data shows correlation, the fine-tuning prices are token rates, and Niobia's funding and customers are undisclosed. If you're a bootstrapped founder, the actionable step is to find the one niche where you already understand the failure cost better than anyone else.
